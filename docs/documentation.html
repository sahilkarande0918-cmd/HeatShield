The one-minute version

When a heatwave hits an Indian city, the government issues one warning for the whole city. But a city is not one temperature. A crowded lane with tin roofs and no trees can be 4–6 °C hotter than a leafy neighbourhood three kilometres away.

HeatShield splits a city into 1 km squares, scores how dangerous each square is, updates that every day using the live weather forecast, and then answers the question a city official actually has to answer:

“I have five water tankers. Where do I send them?”

Three words that appear on every page

Grid cell

One square of the city, roughly 1 km × 1 km. Pune is cut into 744 of them. Every number in this tool is calculated per cell, not per city.

Vulnerability

How bad heat would be here — because of buildings, missing trees and how many people live here. It does not change day to day.

Risk

Vulnerability plus today's actual weather. This is the number that changes daily and the one the map shows.

The one sentence to remember: heat mapping in India is already solved by several good projects — heat action is not. HeatShield is built to close that gap.

HeatShield Documentation03 · The Problem
Why this needs to exist

One alert for a city that is not
one temperature

Heat is one of India's fastest-growing climate killers, and the response has a specific, fixable weakness: it is issued at city scale, while the danger exists at street scale.

When the weather department forecasts 42 °C for Pune, that is one number for 3.1 million people spread across 700-odd square kilometres. Inside that city:

  • A dense lane of tin-roofed houses with no tree cover traps heat and releases it slowly all night. It can run several degrees hotter than the forecast.
  • A planned neighbourhood with parks and older trees can run cooler than the forecast.
  • The hot pockets are usually where the people least able to cope live — outdoor workers, the elderly, families without a fan or a cooler.

So the alert is technically correct and operationally useless. It tells a ward officer that today is dangerous. It does not tell them which street.

This is not a theoretical problem

Ahmedabad built South Asia's first Heat Action Plan in 2013 after a deadly 2010 heatwave. It is credited with averting roughly 1,100 deaths a year, and it worked well enough that India's National Disaster Management Authority asked 23 heat-prone states to build their own. Those plans save lives — and they still mostly stop at a citywide alert. The intra-city targeting step was never built.

Figure 1 · The gap

Same city, same hour. The warning is uniform; the danger is not.

Why this particular gap is worth attacking

The danger is predictable

Unlike an earthquake, a heatwave is forecast days ahead. The information to act early already exists — it is simply not resolved finely enough to act locally.

The hot spots barely move

Buildings and missing trees do not change week to week. The expensive half of the calculation can be done once and reused every day after.

The response is already funded

Cities already own tankers, halls and staff. This is not a request for more money — it is a question of aiming what already exists.

HeatShield Documentation04 · Who It Is For
Audience

Two groups: who uses it,
and who it protects

Group 1 · The users

People who decide where resources go

They open the dashboard. They are not data scientists — they are administrators with a budget, a map and a bad week ahead.

  • Municipal disaster management cells — the office that actually deploys tankers, opens relief centres and issues local warnings.
  • City health departments — who need to know which health posts to stock and which neighbourhoods to send ASHA workers into.
  • Ward officers — the person on the ground with four tankers and two community halls, deciding on a Monday morning.
  • Urban planners — who can see, over time, which wards keep appearing in the danger band and need trees or shade structures.
Group 2 · The beneficiaries

People who never open the app

They benefit because relief arrives closer to them. They are the reason the scoring weights population the way it does.

  • Outdoor workers — construction labour, street vendors, delivery riders, traffic police. Working through the hottest hours is not optional for them.
  • Elderly residents, especially those living alone, whose bodies regulate temperature poorly and who may not seek help.
  • Infants and pregnant women, both at elevated medical risk in extreme heat.
  • Households without cooling — where a fan is the only defence and a power cut removes even that.

HeatShield Documentation05 · How It Works
System architecture

The whole system, in one picture

Read it left to right. Two kinds of information come in on the left, they are combined in the middle, and three useful things come out on the right.

What happens when you click a square

The map never asks you to trust a number you cannot open up. Clicking any square shows the whole chain that produced its score:

  • The risk band in plain words — “Severe: relief centres should open, restrict outdoor work 12:00–16:00.”
  • How many people live there, and what it feels like versus the regional forecast.
  • The three ingredients — built-up density, green cover, distance to water — each as a bar, so you can see why this square scores what it does.
  • A button to draft the ward advisory for that exact neighbourhood.

Why it stays fast

The static layer is read from memory rather than fetched per request, and the forecast is cached for an hour. Dragging the time slider recomputes 744 squares and redraws the map in a few hundred milliseconds.

If the weather service is slow or down, the app serves the last good forecast and labels it “cached” rather than showing a spinner or, worse, a blank map.

HeatShield Documentation06 · The Risk Score
The maths, without the jargon

How one square gets its score

Step 1 — What is built here (done once)

For each 1 km square we count three things from free public data:

BuildingsHow many building footprints are inside the square. More concrete means more heat stored and released at night.
Green coverHow much of the square is park, forest or grass. Trees cool by shading and evaporation.
Water nearbyHow close the nearest lake or river is. Water cools its surroundings, but only for about a kilometre.

These are blended into a single heat proxy, then every square is ranked against every other square in the same city.

Step 2 — Who is here

The city's Census population is spread across the squares in proportion to how many buildings each one has. A square with 5% of the city's buildings gets about 5% of its people. This is a standard technique called dasymetric mapping.

Heat proxy and population density are combined into vulnerability, scored 0–100. This never changes day to day.

Step 3 — Add today's weather

Three sub-steps, and this is where the number becomes live:

  • The forecast is a regional number, so each square gets an urban heat island bump of up to +4 °C, scaled by how built-up it is. That gives a “feels like here” temperature.
  • That temperature is scored against the internationally used heat index danger bands — below 27 °C is no risk, 41 °C is “danger”, 54 °C is the top of the published scale.
  • The result is then weighted by vulnerability, because the same 45 °C is a worse emergency in a crowded block than on an empty ridge.

Real output from a cell in Pune. Every one of these numbers is shown in the app when you click a square — the tool never asks you to trust a score you cannot open up.

Why ranking, not raw values

Our first attempt scaled scores between the lowest and highest building count. Because a few squares have enormous counts, that pushed the median square to 76/100 — the whole city painted “severe”, which is exactly the failure this project exists to fix. Ranking each square against the others instead gives a proper spread: in Pune the 10th, 50th and 90th percentile squares now score 12, 50 and 91.

A colour choice that matters

The scale gets steadily brighter as risk rises, not just redder. That means it still works for red–green colourblind readers and in black-and-white printouts — which the usual green-to-red heat map does not.

HeatShield Documentation07 · The Optimizer
The differentiating feature

Where should the water go?

Every other part of this project exists to make this question answerable. This is the piece comparable tools do not attempt.

The idea in one paragraph

You have a city full of squares, each with a risk score and a population. You already have some relief — public water points. And you have a budget: say five new places you could set one up. The optimizer tries to pick the five that bring the most currently-unreached at-risk people within a short walk of relief.

Why “greedy”?

The algorithm takes the single best option available at each step, then re-checks. It does not try every combination — with 744 possible spots that would be billions of comparisons.

For coverage problems of this shape that shortcut is mathematically well understood: it is guaranteed to land within about 63% of the theoretically perfect answer, and it runs in roughly 40 milliseconds. That speed is what lets the map redraw the instant you drag the budget slider, which is what makes it feel like a tool rather than a report.

It is also explainable one site at a time — “this one reaches 41,000 people nobody currently reaches” — which matters when someone has to justify the decision.

One honest assumption, made visible

OpenStreetMap lists 714 hospitals and 450 clinics in Pune. If we counted all of them as “heat relief”, existing coverage jumps to 94% and the whole question looks solved.

But a private clinic is not a cooling shelter, and a hospital is where you go after heatstroke, not to avoid it. So the default counts only the 26 public water points Pune has mapped for 3.1 million people — and the dashboard exposes this as a switch, so anyone can disagree with us and see the other answer.

Written by hand, on purpose

The optimizer is our own TypeScript, not a library and not a separate Python service. It is a small, well-understood algorithm; adding a second language to the deployment would have bought nothing and cost a lot of fragility.

Checks that run on every build

The risk model and the optimizer both ship with assert-based self-checks — including one that fails the build if the colour ramp ever stops getting brighter as risk rises, because that is what keeps it colourblind-safe.

The three things that are genuinely ours

01

The action layer

Existing work ends at a map. We take the map as the input and produce a decision: coordinates, ranked, with the population each reaches.

02

Live, not an atlas

Most hyperlocal heat maps are historical — “where is it usually hot”. Ours answers “where is it dangerous this Friday afternoon”.

03

Built for the last mile

The output is not a dashboard for analysts. It is a paragraph an officer can paste into WhatsApp, naming the real neighbourhood, with three actions for today.

Where we are weaker, and we should say so: SEEDS and IIT Mandi resolve heat at building level using real satellite land-surface temperature. We work at 1 km squares using a proxy. Their science is better; our contribution is the step after the map. A better input layer would make our optimizer better, not redundant.

  • A 72-hour forecast fused into the risk surface, refreshed hourly, with a trend chart.
  • A siting optimizer with a before-and-after coverage comparison.
  • AI-drafted advisories, named to the real neighbourhood, cached per area per day.

What we deliberately do not claim

  • This is a heat proxy, not satellite temperature. Real land-surface temperature needs Google Earth Engine, which needs an approval we chose not to wait on. Every component is stored separately so real data can replace the proxy without rewriting anything.
  • Scores are relative to their own city. A 70 in Pune and a 70 in Ahmedabad are not the same absolute danger. Absolute danger enters through the forecast, not the vulnerability layer.
  • The forecast is Open-Meteo, not IMD. The interface credits it by name rather than implying an official source.
  • Population is estimated, not counted. Census totals spread by building count is a standard method, but it is an estimate.
  • The advisories are machine-drafted. Every one is labelled as such and carries a read-before-sending notice.

Where it goes next

More cities

The pipeline is city-agnostic. Adding a city means running the seed script once — no code changes.

Better inputs

Swap the heat proxy for real satellite land-surface temperature, and ward boundaries for the 1 km grid, and every number gets sharper.

Beyond heat

Map → forecast → optimise → advise is a general pattern. Flooding and air quality fit it without redesign.

In one line: India already knows how to draw a good heat map. HeatShield is an argument that the valuable, unbuilt part is what you do with it the morning after.
